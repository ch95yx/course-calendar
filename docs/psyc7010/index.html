Assessment of Children

Chapters 1–6 plus Appendices K and L. Select a topic.

Chapter 1 · Challenges in assessing children: the process

Effective decision making is the aim of clinical and psychoeducational assessment. A competent evaluator needs testing and measurement, statistics, child development, personality, psychopathology, and intervention knowledge. The book supplements test manuals; it does not replace supervised practice. When special education is being considered, IDEA 2004 requires evaluation of all areas of suspected disability.

Testing versus assessment

  • Psychological testing administers and scores tests. The focus is collecting data.
  • Psychological assessment is broader: formal and informal tests, observations, and interviews. The focus is integrating, interpreting, and synthesizing the evaluation.

Seven types of assessment

  • Screening: brief. Identifies risk, eligibility, a disorder that may need remediation, or the need for a fuller evaluation.
  • Focused (problem-solving): a detailed look at one area of functioning.
  • Diagnostic: strengths and weaknesses across several areas (cognitive, academic, language, behavioral, personality, emotional and social, sometimes neuropsychological).
  • Counseling and rehabilitation: ability to adjust and carry out daily responsibilities.
  • Progress evaluation: change over days, weeks, months, or years.
  • Curriculum-based: progress in reading, math, writing, and spelling.
  • School function: participation in school activities, need for supports, and ability to function at school.

Four pillars

  • Norm-referenced measures are standardized on a defined norm group. Each score is a rank in that group. They sample behavior economically, quantify functioning, and can show developmental change, cognitive or neurological change, and effects of intervention.
  • Interviews: unstructured (open-ended, no fixed agenda, aimed at the problem), semistructured (a list, but the focus can change), structured (rigid and comprehensive, usually for a psychiatric diagnosis).
  • Observations during testing and in natural settings (class, playground, home).
  • Informal assessment supplements norm-referenced tests when needed.

Multimethod assessment

  • Sources: referral source, child, parents, teachers, others, plus records (educational, medical, social work, prior evaluations).
  • Techniques: the four pillars.
  • Domains: intelligence, attention and memory, processing speed, planning and organization, achievement, visual, auditory, motor, oral language, adaptive behavior, and social-emotional-personality functioning.
  • Findings are used to develop interventions. The work is collaborative.
  • Informed consent and informed refusal: families (and the child, when age and ability allow) must understand the process and what happens to the information before they decide.

Guidelines that quizzes target

  • Do not focus only on numbers. Ask what the results suggest about competencies and limits.
  • Assessment should benefit the child. In court, the goal is objective data and conclusions for the court.
  • Use standard conditions except for documented special circumstances, and score by the manual.
  • Scores can be lowered by limited English, fatigue, hunger, anxiety, stress, poor cooperation, low motivation, inattention, language or memory or executive deficits, temperament, illness, and culture.
  • Interpret a test only for what it was designed to measure, and only together with history, culture, and primary language.
  • Tests need adequate reliability and validity for the use you make of them.
  • Nomothetic: compare the child with others. Idiographic: read the child’s own profile.
  • Interpretation uses both inductive and deductive reasoning.
  • Conclusions rest on all sources, not one piece. Measures of the “same” skill can still disagree.
  • Data are samples of behavior at one time and place. They support inferences about traits; they do not reveal traits directly.
  • Reevaluate after intervention or when IDEA requires it.
  • The halo effect is overgeneralizing from limited information.
  • Choose words carefully in reports and in conversation.

Eleven steps and Endrew F.

  1. Review referral information.
  2. Decide whether to accept the referral.
  3. Obtain background from questionnaires and prior records.
  4. Interview the child, parents, teachers, and relevant others.
  5. Observe the child in several settings.
  6. Select and administer the battery.
  7. Interpret the results.
  8. Develop intervention strategies and recommendations.
  9. Write the report.
  10. Meet with the child (if appropriate), parents, and other concerned people.
  11. Monitor whether the recommendations are effective.

Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District (2017): every IEP must set out a program reasonably calculated to enable the child to receive educational benefit.

Chapter 2 · Challenges in assessing children: the context

Assessment sits inside schools, families, law, theory, and the testing situation. Results describe one child at one time and place. Assessment is a means to decisions that benefit the child, not an end in itself.

IDEA and who is served

The 13 categories are autism, deaf-blindness, developmental delay, emotional disturbance, hearing impairment, intellectual disability, multiple disabilities, orthopedic impairment, other health impairment, specific learning disability, speech or language impairment, traumatic brain injury, and visual impairment. Giftedness and bilingualism are not IDEA disability categories. ADHD is not its own category.

Classification and labeling

  • Categorical systems use discrete labels. Dimensional systems place traits on continua.
  • Labels can aid communication, research, services, legal protection, and a non-blame explanation. They can also create self-fulfilling prophecies and stereotype threat (the risk of conforming to a stereotype about one’s group), excuse behavior, hide individual differences, and pull attention toward the label instead of intervention.
  • Large, dramatic teacher-expectancy effects on IQ have been disconfirmed. Expectancies often predict outcomes because they are fairly accurate.
  • Mild intellectual developmental disability is defined in part by IQ about 55–69, and moderate by about 40–54. Those bands are arbitrary cutoffs.

Theoretical perspectives

  • Developmental: genes and environment interact in a directed way toward walking, talking, complex thought, and social maturity. Rate and timing differ. Look at differences between children and changes within a child.
  • Normative-developmental: compare the child with age expectations. Includes attachment.
  • Attachment (Bowlby): children attach to a limited number of caregivers. Security depends on caregiver sensitivity and responsiveness. Secure attachment supports coping with stress. Types: secure (little distress when the caregiver leaves), ambivalent (distress when the caregiver leaves), avoidant (avoids the caregiver or shows no preference over a stranger), disorganized (unclear attachment; mixed avoidance and resistance; confused or apprehensive).
  • Ecological-transactional: behavior is shaped by nested settings and stressors.
  • Cognitive-behavioral: cognitions, antecedents, and consequences. Functional behavioral assessment asks what maintains a behavior.
  • Family-systems: boundaries. Balanced/clear boundaries are distinct but connected. Enmeshed/diffuse boundaries are overly involved. Disengaged/rigid boundaries are overly distant. Other patterns include intrusive, role-reversal, and spousification boundaries. Boundary problems raise risk of internalizing disorders (for example depression) and externalizing disorders (for example oppositional-defiant behavior).
  • Social-cognitive: attention, retention, production, and motivation; internal versus external locus of control; reciprocal determinism; learned helplessness; learning by watching others; delaying gratification.
  • Sociocultural: development moves from the interpsychological level (between people) to the intrapsychological level (within the child). Development is plastic.
  • Neurodevelopmental: brain development, including altered brain function.
  • Eclectic: more than one of these perspectives, matched to the child.

Why a child may score poorly

  • Individual: neuropsychological, physical, experiential, and temperament factors.
  • Environmental: school, home, peer group, and neighborhood.
  • Causes may be temporary, long-lasting, or permanent. Describe how the child performed under the conditions you actually used.
  • The assessment situation also includes the referral reason, setting, social desirability, reactive effects, evaluator–child variables, and previous testing.

Computers, Q-interactive, and telepsychology

  • Q-interactive is Pearson’s tablet platform for administration, scoring, and interpretation. It uses the standard paper norms. Pearson’s own studies support score equivalence; independent research has not shown the same equivalence.
  • Graduate students (N = 46) made more WISC-V errors with standard paper administration than with digital administration.
  • For Coding and Symbol Search, give the paper response booklet, then transfer the scores onto Q-interactive.
  • Q-global is Pearson’s online scoring and report system. Riverside’s system is Riverside Score.
  • Computer reports save time and calculate accurately, but they cost money, need validation, and omit information you alone have. Review every line. Use them only if you know the test. You are legally and ethically responsible for the statements and for decisions based on them.
  • Telepsychology delivers services by phone, video, email, chat, text, or the internet. When tests are given this way, it is remote online administration. Equivalence studies are mixed. Say in the report that the session was remote and whether a facilitator was on site. Empirical support for teleassessment is still insufficient.
  • The Affordable Care Act pushed electronic health records; HIPAA sets privacy rules for them. Cloud storage is convenient and creates confidentiality risk.

Chapter 3 · Culturally and linguistically diverse children

The book’s preferred term is culturally and linguistically diverse children. Groups are not uniform. Recent immigrants and U.S.-born members of the same ethnicity can differ sharply, and acculturation patterns differ even among people who are acculturated.

Core definitions

  • Culture is the human-made part of the environment: learned, shared systems of meaning, transmitted across generations. Foundations include a common history, a style of communication, relationship patterns, shared values and customs, a geographic home, and patterns of dress and diet. Culture is intertwined with ethnicity and social class, but it is not the same thing as either.
  • Ethnicity: shared heritage, nationality, culture, language, physical features, family names, customs, and religion.
  • Race: defined as a supposed common genetic makeup. The book says it is not meaningful to describe human differences this way, and does not use the term except in quotations (p. 68).
  • Racism: oppression of an ethnic group by individual actions (interpersonal) or by institutional policy (institutional), preserving the dominant group’s status, wealth, or power.
  • Social class: income, wealth, occupation, or education.
  • Acculturation: cultural change when a person meets another culture. People can keep their heritage and also take on mainstream practices.
  • Test bias: scores that depend on group membership rather than the ability or trait the test claims to measure.
  • Prejudice: a preconceived negative opinion about a person or group.

How culture is learned, and how bias forms

  • Four social-learning processes: observing, sharing, transmitting, and participating.
  • Ethnic socialization: parents pass on values, beliefs, and information about ethnicity.
  • Social identity theory: people sort others into ingroup and outgroup quickly. Ingroup favoritism protects the self.
  • Broad stereotypes miss regional, generational, socioeconomic, and individual differences.
  • Stressors that are common, though not universal, include racism, poverty, acculturation conflict, difficulty with schools and other institutions, and difficulty with Standard English. Possible results include alienation, rejection of social norms, distrust of health care, and fear of police.
  • Children born into poverty are more likely to have prenatal, birth-process, and postnatal problems.

Bilingual assessment, translations, and interpreters

  • Uncritical use of translated tests is not recommended. Translation can change meaning and difficulty.
  • Interpreters have their own challenges and do not automatically make a score equivalent to a standard administration.
  • Accommodations (simplified English, glossaries, enlarged print, extra time, a bilingual dictionary) must be understood as possible changes in what the score means.

Arguments and court cases

  • Against: content said to be biased, use for dead-end placements, and negative teacher expectancies.
  • For: tests can reveal talent and support decisions based on performance rather than ancestry. Recognize the limits, and never use a test in a way that harms a child.
  • Diana v. State Board of Education (1970): linguistically different children tested in the primary language and in English; nonverbal measures; an interpreter if no bilingual evaluator.
  • Larry P. v. Riles: Judge Peckham (1979; Ninth Circuit upheld in 1984) held that standardized intelligence tests were racially and culturally biased and had not been validated for placing Black children in classes for “educable mentally retarded.”
  • PASE v. Hannon (1980): Judge Grady held that the tests were not culturally biased against African American children when used with other criteria, and that Chicago’s process met federal rules.
  • Georgia NAACP / Marshall v. Georgia: no finding of differential treatment in the procedures used to place students in classes for intellectual disability.

Chapter 4 · Role of the evaluator

Competence is manual skill plus child development, psychopathology, emotional steadiness, speed, communication, and knowing the edge of your competence. Anxiety at the start is expected. Procedure becomes routine with practice.

Who can evaluate

  • Academic preparation in school, clinical, counseling, or pediatric psychology.
  • Emotional stability, maturity, and patience with children who have special needs.
  • Speed in recording, handling materials, scoring, and responding to the unexpected.
  • Problem solving, communication, and the ability to work alone and on a team.
  • Ongoing learning, awareness of limits, and refusal to practice beyond competence.
  • Personal qualities that help: tact, ingenuity, patience, understanding, warmth, respect, flexibility, vigilance, humor, and genuine liking for people.
  • Know your own reactions to disability, gender, culture, language, and class. If your physical or emotional state would spoil the session, do not proceed. If you cannot establish rapport, withdraw and refer.

Expectancy and confirmatory bias

  • Impressions form from the referral, records, appearance, and early items. Do not let them change standardized administration.
  • Expectancy / halo effect: an early impression colors later judgments.
  • Confirmatory bias: seeking evidence that fits the hypothesis and ignoring evidence that does not.
  • Binet, Wechsler, and standardization were designed to reduce expectancy effects. Each evaluator still has to prevent them.
  • Body language and eye contact communicate interest. Dress to the professional norms of the setting.

Preparation and administration

  • A school prereferral team may decide whether an assessment is needed and what kind.
  • Study the referral. Clarify it if it is vague. Request outside records early because consent and delivery take time. Review unfamiliar manuals and current literature.
  • Before testing, check vision, hearing, glasses, and hearing aids.
  • Watch effort, offer encouragement, and break for fatigue. Follow standard procedures even when you are flexible about pacing.
  • Lay out materials the same way each time. For block-design demonstrations, complete one row at a time from the child’s left to the child’s right, unless the manual says otherwise.
  • Queries clarify a response. They do not teach or improve a wrong answer. Ask them in a nonthreatening way.
  • Praise effort rather than signaling which answers were correct.
  • A published reliability and validity argument applies to children like those in the standardization sample. Limited English familiarity, for example, can make the score a poor measure of the ability you intended.

Testing the limits and special needs

  • Testing the limits gives graduated help, more time, or extra cues to see what the child can do with support. Other names: interactive assessment, process-oriented assessment, dynamic assessment, mediated learning, the instruction-oriented method, and graduated prompts.
  • Do this after the standardized administration. Aided performance is not the standard score.
  • Do not confuse a sensory or motor deficit with low intelligence. Adapt the situation, and record every departure from the manual so the reader knows the score is not fully standard.

Chapter 5 · History and theories of intelligence

Shared elements in definitions of intelligence include adaptation, basic processes, and higher-order thinking: abstract reasoning, problem solving, acquiring knowledge, memory, mental speed, and linguistic competence. Western views stress logic and problem solving. Many Eastern views stress the holistic, social ability to see complexity and contradiction. An IQ is a somewhat arbitrary summary of many abilities, always modified by experience.

History worth memorizing

  • Esquirol: intellectual incapacity (abilities never developed) versus mental illness (abilities lost or unusable).
  • Galton: father of the psychometric testing movement; objective methods; regression to the mean and correlation. He emphasized simple sensory tasks.
  • Pearson: product-moment correlation, multiple and partial correlation, phi, and chi-square.
  • German contributors include Wundt, Kraepelin, Ebbinghaus, Wernicke, and Ziehen.
  • James McKeen Cattell (1890) first used the term “mental test.”
  • Wissler and Sharp reported discouraging validity for early tests and briefly slowed the field. Their methods were weak.
  • Binet, Henri, and Simon measured higher mental processes rather than simple sensation. The 1905 Binet–Simon Scale helped create clinical psychology. The years about 1880–1905 are the “laboratory” period that fed applied psychology.
  • Goddard (1908) introduced the 1905 scale in the United States.
  • Terman (1916 Stanford–Binet) adopted Stern’s mental quotient and named it the IQ: (mental age / chronological age) × 100. Revisions with Merrill in 1937 and 1960.
  • Yerkes proposed a point-scale format (items grouped by type and scored by points) rather than only an age scale.
  • Wechsler built the Wechsler–Bellevue from 11 modified published tests used as subtests.

Factor-analytic camps

  • Factor results depend on the tests, the method, and the names the investigator chooses. Labels describe patterns. They do not by themselves prove biological entities.
  • Thorndike: many interconnected but distinct abilities.
  • Thurstone: intelligence is organized and is not one unitary trait (primary mental abilities).
  • Guilford’s structure of intellect: three dimensions — operations, contents, and products.
  • Cattell and Horn: fluid intelligence (Gf) is novel, adaptive, relatively culture-reduced efficiency. Crystallized intelligence (Gc) is acquired knowledge and skill, culture-specific, and more tied to education, personality, and experience. Horn rejected a unitary g.
  • Spearman: a general factor g plus specific factors s.
  • Vernon: g, then two major group factors, verbal-educational and spatial-mechanical, then narrower factors.
  • Carroll’s three strata: narrow abilities (I), broad abilities (II), and g (III). The broad level in his model includes fluid, crystallized, general memory and learning, broad visual perception, broad auditory perception, broad retrieval, broad cognitive speediness, and processing speed.
  • CHC theory integrates Cattell–Horn with Carroll: narrow (I), broad (II), general (III). The list of broad abilities has changed as the model evolved.

Other theories

  • Information processing (Campione, Brown, Borkowski): an architectural system (capacity, durability, efficiency) and an executive system (knowledge base, schemes, control processes, metacognition).
  • Sternberg’s triarchic / successful intelligence: componential (analytic; internal processing, including metacomponents, performance components, and knowledge-acquisition components), experiential (creative; connecting internal processes to the world, including novelty and automatization), and contextual (practical; adapting to, shaping, or selecting environments).
  • PASS (Das, Naglieri, Kirby): planning, attention, simultaneous processing, and successive processing.
  • Gardner: at least eight relatively autonomous intelligences — linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, intrapersonal, interpersonal, and naturalist.
  • Ceci’s bioecological theory: (1) multiple cognitive abilities, (2) gene–environment interplay across development changes intelligence, (3) processes depend on context, (4) noncognitive traits matter because they shape experience.
  • Piaget’s periods, in order: sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational. Assimilation fits experience into existing schemes (p. 176). Accommodation changes schemes to fit new experience. Organization and adaptation are the broader processes. Piaget stresses new structures. Psychometrics stresses the normal curve and differences between people. Both accept genetic and maturational influences.
  • Perkins’s learnable intelligence: neural, experiential, and reflective.
  • Dweck (p. 179): everyone has an implicit theory. The entity view says intelligence is fixed: you are born with a set amount. The incremental view says intelligence can grow with effort. Entity beliefs orient the person toward looking competent and avoiding challenge when success is uncertain. Incremental beliefs orient the person toward learning.
  • P-FIT: frontal, parieto-temporal, and cingulate networks as a biological basis of intelligence.
  • Process overlap theory: working memory, executive attention, fluid reasoning, and crystallized knowledge overlap to produce general mental ability.

Chapter 6 · Correlates of intelligence

Intelligence tests sample abilities the culture values: verbal ability, concepts, judgment, reasoning, memory, comprehension, speed, and spatial skill. They predict school and work reasonably well and do not measure all of intelligent behavior. The ethical pressure point is that scores are used to allocate scarce resources. Read the IQ in the context of the whole child.

Brain

  • The brain is about 2% of body weight and about 25% of basal metabolism. No two brains are identical. More than half of human genes help build the brain.
  • Gray matter processes information. White matter connects processing centers. Both relate to patterns of intellectual strength.
  • Demanding working-memory and reasoning tasks recruit frontal cortex. The right frontal region is linked more with integrating verbal and spatial information; the left with nonspatial memory.
  • Cortical efficiency (p. 188): more intelligent people tend to show faster neurological functioning and less metabolic activity (less blood flow and glucose uptake) in the areas doing the work. Higher intelligence is also linked to healthier neurons and glia. Education, mental and physical stimulation, aerobic exercise, and antioxidant-rich food can support efficiency.
  • Brain volume (p. 189): modestly related to intelligence. Three meta-analyses span about r = .21 to .33 (summary, p. 212): McDaniel r = .33, Rushton and Ankney r = .21, Pietschnig r = .24. One older-adult sample found r = .31.
  • Neural tube (p. 189): begins about 16 days after conception and closes about day 28, then becomes brain and spinal cord. The brainstem is largely mature by the end of the second trimester. The cortex begins to function at birth and matures last.
  • Myelination is fastest in the first 2 years, is largely hard-wired, and is substantially disrupted mainly by severe malnutrition. Association cortex continues myelinating into the second and third decades.
  • Approximate brain weight: 400 g at birth, 1,000 g at 1 year, about 90% of the adult weight (about 1,400 g) by age 5.
  • Brain reserve and cognitive reserve refer to biological and functional resilience.

Heredity

  • Heritability is the proportion of variation in a trait, in a population, associated with genetic differences. It runs from 0 to 1. It does not apply to one child, does not say where that child’s intelligence came from, and does not mean the score is fixed (p. 191).
  • The best overall estimate for intelligence is about .50: about half of the differences among people, with the other half associated with nongenetic factors. Because half is not genetic, some environmental influence is possible.
  • Estimates come from kinship designs (identical twins from one egg; fraternal twins from two eggs; adoptions). Human mating and environments cannot be experimentally controlled, so the figures are estimates.
  • Genotype is genetic makeup. Phenotype is the observed characteristic.
  • Genetic influence increases with age (p. 199): about 20% in infancy, 40% in childhood, and about 80% by ages 18–20, then levels off. Environmental influence falls to about 10% by ages 18–20.
  • Assortative mating means partners resemble each other on a trait. The generalist genes hypothesis says many of the same genes influence many cognitive abilities.

Environment, schooling, and the Flynn effect

  • Prenatal and early risks include malnutrition, lead and other toxins, infections such as cytomegalovirus and toxoplasmosis, maternal stress and cortisol, low birth weight, prematurity, and perinatal complications.
  • School quality accounts for only about 2% to 10% of variance in children’s cognitive scores. Years of schooling and IQ correlate about r = .69 (p. 197). That correlation cannot show which causes which. Each extra year of education is associated with about 1 to 3.7 points on expected adult IQ. Each year of high school missed is associated with about 1.8 points lower.
  • Flynn effect (p. 198): Flynn reported gains of about 3 IQ points per decade, larger on nonverbal tests and concentrated in the lower part of the distribution. A later meta-analysis found about 0.22 point per year. Proposed reasons include schooling, nutrition, smaller families, test sophistication, and a more stimulating environment.

Gender

  • Overall IQ: essentially no difference. Tests are built to remove biased items, and specific strengths offset. Women have a higher percentage of gray matter and men a higher percentage of white matter; more white matter is involved in intellectual activity in women.
  • Verbal: small differences. Females tend to acquire language earlier, develop larger vocabularies, do better on many language tasks including writing, and have fewer reading and stuttering difficulties. Comprehension/knowledge scores are similar.
  • Math: essentially no difference in general concepts at any age. Females tend to be somewhat higher on computation in elementary and middle school, not in high school. By the end of grade 12, males are slightly stronger in problem solving and geometry.
  • Spatial: males tend to be better at visual-spatial ability, spatial perception (horizontality or verticality), and mental rotation. Spatial visualization (finding a simple figure inside a complex one) is similar.
  • Memory: small differences. Females tend to remember word lists, personal events, novel associations such as names and faces, and spatial locations. Males tend to remember factual material in math, science, and mechanics.
  • Variability: males’ scores spread more.

Speed, school, stability, and other correlates

  • Faster simple, choice, and complex reaction time, and shorter inspection time, relate to higher intelligence. Temporal resolution (how finely someone times events) also relates to intelligence.
  • Executive functions (p. 203): seven often cited are planning and goal setting, organizing, prioritizing, working memory, shifting, inhibition, and self-regulation. Updating working memory is more strongly related to intelligence than inhibiting prepotent responses or shifting mental sets. Working memory and g correlate about r = .48; short-term memory and intelligence about r = .28. Working memory both stores and manipulates; short-term memory mainly stores.
  • Tests in the first two years are poor predictors of later intelligence. By about age 2 the correlation with later scores is only about .40. Scores in early adolescence predict adult intelligence well. Deary’s follow-ups from age 11 into old age found about r = .61 to .78 (median about .73).
  • Constancy is greater when the child is older at the first test and the interval is shorter. Interpret IQs obtained before age 5 cautiously. Individuals still change enough that placement decisions need reevaluation. Do not forecast a child’s future level with confidence.
  • Scores stay relatively stable partly because genes are stable, environments are often stable for a given child, development does not simply reverse, and tests at different ages overlap in the abilities they measure. Absolute level still rises with age as knowledge, abstraction, efficiency, and working memory grow. Some later declines track loss of brain tissue; fluid ability is tied to frontal volume, working memory, speed, abstract rules, and retrieval.
  • Intelligence is positively associated with self-control. Critical thinking is part of cognitive ability.
  • Adolescents with a psychiatric disorder score, on average, about 3 to 6 points below 100.

Appendix K · Ethical, legal, and professional issues

Know the law of your jurisdiction and the ethics code of your profession. Codes set standards and protect the public. A dilemma often has no single obvious action. Ethical duty can go beyond what the statute requires. Awareness of the code is necessary and not sufficient.

Decision making and liability

  1. Recognize a problem.
  2. Define the problem.
  3. Develop solutions.
  4. Choose a solution.
  5. Review the process, including everyone’s values.
  6. Implement and evaluate.
  7. Continue to reflect.

Reduce liability by using tests with demonstrated reliability and validity for the kind of person you are testing, noting exceptions, and keeping accurate records. Violations can bring malpractice claims and discipline, including expulsion from a professional body. If you use a computer-generated interpretation, you remain responsible for it.

Five federal laws

  • IDEA 2004: an education law. Special education and an IEP. Key ideas include a free appropriate public education (FAPE) and the least restrictive environment (LRE).
  • Section 504 and the ADA: civil rights laws. They entitle students with disabilities to accommodations in general education. They do not, by themselves, create an IEP.
  • FERPA (1974): an education law for schools that receive federal funds. Parents have rights over education records. You generally cannot refuse assessment or therapy information to a custodial parent. Release to others usually needs written permission. Schools give an annual notice of rights. Noncompliance can cost federal funds. FERPA covers all students, including students with disabilities.
  • HIPAA: privacy of health information held by health-care providers, including psychologists. Parents usually hold an adolescent’s rights. Exceptions include emancipation and treatment for pregnancy, a sexually transmitted infection, or substance abuse. If a state privacy law is stricter, the state law wins. If the state law is weaker, HIPAA wins.
  • The U.S. Department of Education enforces Section 504, the ADA, IDEA, and FERPA in public schools.

Confidentiality, privilege, and maltreatment

  • Confidentiality: do not reveal information from a professional relationship without consent of the client or legal representative.
  • Privileged communication: a legal status. Designated relationships (attorney, spouse, clergy, psychologist, psychiatrist, and others depending on the state) often cannot be forced to disclose in court. The communication generally must have been private.
  • Limits: reasonable suspicion of child maltreatment; a minor’s threat to self; a physical threat to another person; delay of medical care that would risk the child’s health; treatment needed to reduce a minor’s physical pain. The theme is preventing serious, foreseeable, imminent harm.
  • Schools, clinics, and hospitals cannot promise absolute secrecy, because the team sees the record. Say so.
  • Mandatory reporters (mental health professionals, school staff, health workers, child-care providers, and others) report when a reasonable person in that role would suspect maltreatment. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt is not the standard.
  • Four types: physical abuse, neglect, sexual abuse, and emotional abuse.
  • If a child discloses: listen, follow up as needed, do not selectively reinforce, write the words down, and notify the authority.

Consent and forensic work

  • Informed consent from the parent comes before assessment. Seek the child’s assent even when the child’s legal consent is not required. Explain the nature and purpose. Divorce complicates who may consent.
  • Clinical work is usually requested by parents, schools, or health providers and aims to help the client.
  • Forensic work is usually requested by attorneys, courts, child-protection agencies, or insurers. The evaluator is a fact finder. The work may be used in litigation and will often be cross-examined. Keep meticulous notes. Opinions must follow all of the data, not pressure from the retaining attorney. Agree the role clearly before you accept the case.

Regulation and degrees

  • Boards screen applicants, set entry standards, investigate complaints, and discipline violations.
  • Licensure rests on education, supervised experience, and professional knowledge. A license in one jurisdiction does not automatically allow practice in another.
  • The entry degree for independent practice in most U.S. and Canadian jurisdictions is a doctorate. PhD: scientist-practitioner training. PsyD: more emphasis on clinical practice, with research methods still included. EdD: an education doctorate covering research, counseling, development, administration, and assessment. Most boards do not allow full independent practice on a master’s alone.
  • A master’s in school psychology from a NASP-approved program can lead to the Nationally Certified School Psychologist credential.
  • NASP standards exist to protect the public and the people who receive school psychological services. The Code of Fair Testing Practices covers selecting tests, administering and scoring them, and reporting and interpreting results. APA record-keeping guidelines cover keeping, maintaining, and disposing of records.

Appendix L · Statistics and psychometrics

Measurement assigns numbers by rules. Psychological attributes are less tangible than length or weight, and the scores still carry meaning. Statistics shrink data, communicate standing, and estimate error. No score is free of error, and validity always belongs to a particular use.

Scales of measurement

  • Nominal: named categories with no meaningful order. Fits discrete variables.
  • Ordinal: rank, without equal distances. Also discrete. Percentile ranks behave this way.
  • Interval: order plus equal units and an arbitrary zero (Celsius temperature). Continuous.
  • Ratio: equal units and a true zero, so ratios such as “twice as long” are meaningful. Continuous.
  • Interval and ratio scales also have the properties of the scales below them.

Description and correlation

  • Mean: arithmetic average. Median: middle score when ordered. Mode: most frequent score; useful for nominal data. More than one mode means the distribution is multimodal.
  • Range: highest minus lowest. Variance: spread around the mean. Standard deviation: typical distance from the mean, in the original units. A normal distribution graphs as a bell curve.
  • A correlation gives strength (absolute value) and direction. Higher |r| improves prediction. It does not prove cause. A large sample can make a weak r “significant.”
  • Pearson’s r (p. 409) needs continuous, roughly normal variables, a linear relationship, few outliers, and similar spread. If those fail (for example, ordinal data), the Spearman rank-difference correlation is a common alternative (p. 410). A linear r underestimates a curvilinear relationship.
  • Rough labels used in the appendix: .20–.29 low, .30–.49 moderately low, .50–.69 moderate, .70–.79 moderately high, .80–.99 high.
  • r² is the coefficient of determination (p. 411), the shared variance. The book’s example is r = .60, so r² = .36 (36%). A quiz item that uses r = .50 is the same idea: 25%.
  • Mediators explain how or why. Moderators explain when. Multiple R relates one variable to two or more others and runs from .00 to +1.00. The standard error of estimate is the standard deviation of prediction errors.
  • Correcting for attenuation estimates the correlation if both measures were perfectly reliable. Real measures are never perfect, so the corrected value can inflate the relationship.

Derived scores

  • Criterion-referenced: versus an objective standard. Standards-referenced: versus defined standards of quality. Norm-referenced: versus a norm group.
  • Population: the whole group. Representative sample: mirrors that population. Random sample: each person has an equal chance. Reference group: the norm group used to compute standard scores and percentiles. Match norms on age, grade, gender, region, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, and note when they were collected.
  • z: mean 0, SD 1 (p. 414). T: mean 50, SD 10; T = 10(z) + 50 (p. 415). Deviation-IQ-style standard score: mean 100, SD 15; SS = 15(z) + 100.
  • Percentile rank: the percent of the norm group at or below that score. Units are not equal. Quartiles split the group into four 25% bands. Deciles split it into ten 10% bands.
  • NCE: mean 50, SD 21.06 (p. 416). Stanine: mean 5, SD 2, whole numbers 1 through 9.
  • Age and grade equivalents are average raw scores at an age or grade. They are psychometrically impure: unequal units, they exaggerate small raw-score gaps, and a grade equivalent does not mean the child belongs in that grade. Ratio IQ = (MA / CA) × 100 has poor properties; deviation IQs replaced it.
  • Every derived score starts from a raw score. There is no single correct way to express it.

Reliability and error

  • Classical theory: obtained score = true score + error score. The true score is hypothetical. Reliability coefficients (rxx) run from .00 to 1.00.
  • Internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha is the most general index): one administration. Test-retest: the same test twice (stability). Alternate forms: two similar forms. Interrater: agreement between scorers.
  • Reliability tends to rise with a longer, more homogeneous test, a suitable retest interval, greater score variability, less guessing, a stable testing situation, and an adequate sample.
  • The standard error of measurement is the standard deviation of error scores. Lower reliability means a larger SEM. Report a confidence interval. For questions about how the child is functioning now, the book recommends the interval around the obtained score, not the estimated true score.
  • Practice effects can come from prior exposure, events between tests, the type of task, and the population. Regression to the mean: high scores often contained positive error and low scores negative error, so retest scores move toward the mean.
  • Generalizability theory asks how well one sample of behavior represents behavior across conditions (called facets).

Validity, items, and factors

  • Content validity: items represent the domain. Face validity: the test looks relevant; this is weak evidence. Construct validity: it measures the intended trait. Convergent evidence: same-trait measures agree. Discriminant evidence: different-trait measures do not correlate highly.
  • Criterion-related: the test correlates with an outcome. Concurrent means the criterion is collected now. Predictive means it is collected later.
  • Item difficulty: percent correct. Item discrimination: how well the item separates high and low total scores. Item response theory uses difficulty, discrimination, and guessing.
  • Differential item functioning: an item is easier for one group than another at the same ability. It is uniform when that gap is similar at all ability levels.
  • Classification against a criterion yields a true positive (hit), false positive (false alarm), false negative (miss: the test says absent and the condition is present), and true negative. Judge accuracy against the base rate, the rate of the condition in that population.
  • A conventional significance level is 5%. Effect size (Cohen’s d) is the mean difference in standard-deviation units and does not depend on sample size. Significance is not the same thing as a meaningful difference.
  • Factor analysis explains correlations with fewer factors. Exploratory factor analysis has no prior structure. Confirmatory factor analysis tests a hypothesized structure. Variance splits into communality (shared factors), specificity (unique to that variable), and error variance (1 − reliability). Naming factors is a judgment. Method, number of factors, and rotation can change the result.
  • Two tests with the same name can still disagree because of floors, ceilings, item gradients, norm tables, reliability, content, publication date, or sampling.
  • Child factors that spoil validity include anxiety, fatigue, illness, inattention, rapport, motivation, language, hearing, and unfamiliarity with the materials. If you doubt the result, say so in the report.
